2. Executive Summary
PEHNO must not look or behave like a reskinned marketplace. The experience should feel like a modern Indian fashion-tech product whose core job is helping a customer move from uncertainty to confidence. The interface should be visual and aspirational during discovery, factual and comparison-oriented during evaluation, and calm and distraction-free during checkout.
The design system therefore separates three customer mental modes: Explore, Decide, and Buy. Each mode has its own density, information hierarchy, and interaction priorities while remaining visually consistent.
Buyer psychology is used here to reduce cognitive load, surface trustworthy evidence, preserve user control, and make important actions easy to find. It must not be used to create fake urgency, hidden charges, forced subscriptions, confirm-shaming, or other manipulative patterns.

4. Buyer Psychology Framework
PEHNO should use established usability and decision-science ideas as design constraints rather than as persuasion tricks. The objective is to make a correct decision easier, not to pressure a customer into a purchase.
Psychology / usability concept
PEHNO design rule
Example
Cognitive load reduction
Group related choices and avoid showing every option at once.
Price, size, color and delivery are grouped; advanced filters sit behind "More filters".
Hick-Hyman principle
Reduce the number of equally prominent choices at a decision point.
One primary CTA; 5–7 top navigation choices; related actions moved to menus.
Fitts's law
Important touch/click targets must be large and easy to reach.
Primary CTAs >= 44 px touch height on mobile; generous spacing between destructive actions.
Gestalt proximity / similarity
Elements that belong together should look and sit together.
Price + discount + final payable amount live in one pricing block.
Von Restorff isolation
A truly important action may be visually distinct.
Try On uses the brand peach treatment; checkout uses one high-contrast primary CTA.
Social proof
Show authentic review evidence near the product decision.
Rating, review count, verified-purchase signal and photo reviews.
Uncertainty reduction
Answer the customer's strongest unknown before checkout.
Delivery date, returns, seller reliability, fit and price history are visible.
Choice architecture
Organize choices so the easiest path is clear without hiding alternatives.
"Best match for you" can be highlighted while competitors remain visible and comparable.
Loss aversion — used carefully
Allow user-controlled price/stock alerts instead of pressure messaging.
"Notify me if price drops below X" rather than fake urgency.

Design policy: no psychological principle may override truthful information, user consent, accessibility, or the ability to reverse a choice.

6.2 Color usage rules
Do not use coral, peach, green, red, purple, and gold simultaneously in one component. A component should have one dominant role color plus neutral support.
Purple is reserved for AI-originated functionality so customers can quickly distinguish AI from standard commerce controls.
Green means a positive state or verified/completed condition; never use green merely because it "converts well."
Red is reserved for errors, destructive actions, or clearly negative status. Do not use red for ordinary discount labels.
Gold is for ratings/quality signals and selected premium cues. It should not become the default "buy now" color.
Every color-coded state must also include text or icon meaning for accessibility.

12. Accessibility Requirements
Target WCAG 2.2 AA for the experience. Use sufficient text/background contrast and do not rely on color alone to communicate state.
All interactive controls must have visible keyboard focus states.
Touch targets should be at least 44 px high on mobile for primary controls; adjacent controls need enough separation to prevent accidental taps.
All meaningful images need useful alt text; decorative imagery must be marked decorative in implementation.
Product cards must expose product name, price, rating and primary action to assistive technology in logical reading order.
Form errors must be associated with their fields and communicated in text.
Modal dialogs must trap focus, have a clear title, provide an accessible close action, and return focus to the trigger.
Motion must be optional where it may create discomfort; avoid motion that blocks task completion.
Do not use tiny low-contrast helper text for legally or financially important information.

17. Trust, Safety and Privacy UX
Personal images used for try-on must have an explicit storage/processing explanation and a clear delete control.
Avoid retaining user photos longer than the product needs. Backend retention must match the privacy policy.
Seller and product trust signals must be backed by real data; do not invent verification badges.
Sponsored placements must be unmistakably labelled and visually distinguishable from organic results.
Price, discount and delivery claims must use verified source data and timestamps where appropriate.
Returns, cancellations and refunds must be discoverable before purchase and during the order lifecycle.

22. A/B Testing Guardrails
Test one meaningful UX hypothesis at a time when possible.
Measure funnel health and user trust metrics together; a higher click-through rate is not automatically a better experience.
Do not optimize by hiding important fees, obscuring cancellations, or using misleading urgency.
Document hypothesis, audience, success metrics, guardrails, and stop criteria before launch.
For AI features, monitor recommendation quality, correction rate, user override rate and reported errors in addition to conversion.

25. Anti-Patterns — Explicitly Prohibited
Fake countdown timers or fake scarcity.
Preselected paid extras or hidden subscription renewal.
Confusing "Accept" / "Decline" hierarchy designed to force a choice.
Hidden delivery fees revealed only after payment is initiated.
Reviews or ratings presented without clear authenticity context.
AI claims that imply guaranteed fit, guaranteed body transformation, or certainty when evidence is incomplete.
Infinite recommendation carousels with no task relevance.
Using multiple bright accent colors for competing CTAs.
Clearing form data after validation errors.
Designing only desktop first and "making it responsive" at the end.
26. Final Design Checklist for Every Screen
What is the user trying to accomplish on this screen?
What is the single primary action?
Can the customer understand the next step without reading everything?
What uncertainty is the screen removing: price, fit, quality, delivery, seller, return, or choice?
Which elements are AI-generated and are they clearly labelled?
Are important facts grouped using proximity and hierarchy?
Does every interactive control have default, hover, focus, pressed, loading, disabled and error states where applicable?
Can the task be completed with keyboard and assistive technology?
Does the screen remain clear at mobile width?
Does any part of the UI use urgency, scarcity, or social proof in a way that could mislead the customer?

28. Handoff Summary
PEHNO = DISCOVER TRY COMPARE DECIDE BUY

This DRD is the baseline for all future PEHNO UI/UX work. New features should fit the same mental model: help the customer discover the right option, see it in context, understand the tradeoffs, trust the transaction, and complete the purchase with minimal friction.
The visual direction should remain warm, editorial, Indian-rooted and technologically modern. The design should feel clearly different from traditional marketplace interfaces even when it uses familiar commerce patterns underneath.
